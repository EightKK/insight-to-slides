# Capital Equipment: Demand, Delivery, Margins and Cash

**Goal:** produce decision-ready analysis of semiconductor equipment, heavy equipment and other high-value, long-lead-time manufacturers without equating customer intent, firm orders, shipments, recognized sales, profits and cash receipts.

## Guardrails for the evidence chain

1. Separate *market demand*, *customer capex plans*, *expressions of interest*, *firm bookings*, *order backlog*, *capacity*, *shipments*, *revenue recognition* and *cash collection*. These are not interchangeable. Cite every step of a proposed bridge. Never invent order values if the reviewed financial package gives only qualitative order commentary.
2. Distinguish periods and definitions: Q3 vs 9M, quarterly vs cumulative, euros vs USD, percentage growth vs percentage-point margin change, revenue vs system shipments, booked orders vs orders discussed for delivery in later years.
3. Installed-base services: do not infer that an increasing service or upgrade **revenue share** caused improving gross margin unless the share actually rose and there is evidence for the attribution. A higher-margin product **within** the installed-base segment can improve total margin even when segment revenue share falls. Recalculate both the ratio and the movement in margin.
4. Do not present non-operating revaluation gains as recurring operating profit. Identify the P&L level and explain why subtracting the gross valuation figure does not automatically create a GAAP-adjusted net income metric.
5. Downpayments and cash timing: a high quarter's operating cash flow/free cash flow may be driven by advance payments. Avoid forecasting it into an annual run rate without assessing payment timing, working capital and future settlement.
6. Customer validation: cross-check demand with at least one customer capex, technology adoption or revenue disclosure from an independent issuer. Note that the customer's total capex is not the equipment supplier's addressable revenue.
7. Executive assessment: provide a currently favored explanation, at least one viable opposing explanation, a counterfactual and concrete observations that would change the decision. Distinguish company guidance from analyst inference.

## Core checks

- Calculate historical growth from source-unit figures before rounding.
- Recompute ratio: installed-base sales ÷ consolidated sales, on every period being compared.
- Verify that a reported gross-margin change and installed-base revenue-share change have consistent signs. If opposite, explicitly challenge mix-based explanations.
- Use quarter-specific operating cash flow, capex and reported company definition of FCF consistently. An extraordinary cash quarter needs an annotation.
- Present management's supply-capacity statements as forecasts, not measured deficits; avoid fabricating a demand curve.
- Explicitly flag unobserved information in the *internal* evidence ledger. Present audience-facing limits only when relevant to the business decision.

## Demonstration: ASML 2026 Q3

- Q2 2026 gross margin: 54.0%; Q3 2026: 57.5%, +3.5 percentage points.
- Installed-base revenue share: 2,762/9,326 = 29.6% in Q2; 3,289/11,662 = 28.2% in Q3, **−1.4 percentage points**. This rules out a simple explanation based on a larger installed-base revenue share. Management described a highly profitable *upgrade product mix* within the business.
- Q3 2026 reported net income includes about EUR 0.5 billion of Mistral AI investment valuation gain. State the caveat without inventing an adjusted GAAP income figure.
- Q3 2026 free cash flow of about EUR 11.35 billion reflected significant customer downpayments. Its repeatability is not established.
- Customer corroboration: TSMC's Q3 2026 results and FY2026 USD 60–64 billion capex plan are evidence of customer-side investment strength, but not ASML-specific order volumes.

**Evaluation limit:** passing these deterministic checks validates only the numeric and argument structure; it does not prove future demand, supply ramp execution or actual reader comprehension.
